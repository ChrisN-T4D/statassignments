Week 1: Why learn statistics

Start here: see why psychologists need statistics, set up Methods Market, and introduce yourself.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Explain why scientists use statistics as a safeguard against biased/limited human reasoning rather than relying on intuition alone.
  • Define the belief bias effect in logical reasoning.
  • Differentiate deductive validity from the truth or believability of a conclusion (i.e., validity does not require true premises).
  • Interpret overall summaries cautiously by checking subgroup patterns and asking whether aggregated results hide important differences.

Week 2: Research design and measurement

Variables and measurement come first because every later statistic depends on what was measured and how.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Define psychological measurement and distinguish the thing being measured (theoretical construct) from the measurement/observed value.
  • Define operationalisation and distinguish among theoretical construct, measure, operationalisation, and variable.
  • Classify variables by scale of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) and distinguish continuous versus discrete variables.
  • Define reliability and validity, differentiate them, and identify major forms of reliability (test–retest, inter-rater, parallel forms, internal consistency).

Week 3: Evaluate a measure

Apply reliability and validity to a real measure before you start working with data.

Week 4: Software basics

Learn the software now so the next modules can focus on statistics instead of menus.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Identify the main parts of the statistical software's interface and use the basic analysis workflow (select an analysis, set options, view results, remove an analysis).
  • Import/open data files in the statistical software (e.g., its native format, .csv, and other common formats) and verify key import settings (header row, decimal/separator, quoting, missing values).
  • Add optional modules or extensions where needed and save/export work appropriately (export data vs save a project file that includes analyses).
  • Describe how the software represents data (rows as cases, columns as variables) and set an appropriate variable level/type (ID, nominal, ordinal, continuous; text/integer/decimal) based on the data and intended analysis.
  • Create computed variables in the statistical software (e.g., transformations, z-scores, sum-scores, means) and state an appropriate use-case for each in preparing data for analysis.

Week 5: Data handling

Practice entering, importing, and saving data so you can show your work on your own file.

Week 6: Benchmark 1

Benchmark 1 checks Modules 1 to 3. Use the practice test to find what to review first.

Week 7: Descriptive statistics

Describing a data set (center, spread, shape) is the first step of every analysis you will run.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Calculate and interpret mean, median, and mode, and choose a measure of central tendency that fits the scale and shape of the data (including outliers).
  • Calculate and interpret measures of variability (deviation scores, variance, standard deviation, restriction of range) and explain why SD is usually reported instead of variance.
  • Describe skewness and other distribution shapes, and explain when mean and SD can mislead (e.g., skew, bimodality).
  • Compute and interpret z-scores as standardized distances from the mean.
  • Run descriptives in the statistical software and use Compute to create deviation, squared-deviation, or z-score variables.

Week 8: Check normality

Check whether a data set looks normal, because many later tests assume it does.

Week 9: Graphing and visualization

Graphs show what the numbers hide, and you will need both to explain results later.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Choose and interpret graphs for one or two variables (histogram, bar chart, boxplot, scatterplot), including shape, skew, and IQR.
  • Identify misleading visualization choices (truncated axes, bin width, mean±SE vs raw data) and pick a better display.
  • Distinguish frequency tables from contingency tables and say what each is for.
  • Use logical expressions, Compute/Transform, mathematical functions, and filters or case selection to recode, subset, and prepare data.
  • Create histograms, apply filters, and transform or recode variables in the statistical software.

Week 12: Probability and sampling

Probability is the bridge from describing a sample to making claims about a population.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Use the normal distribution and z-scores (e.g., the 68% rule) to describe where scores fall.
  • Apply basic probability ideas (range, long-run frequency, independence vs mutual exclusivity, binomial, frequentist vs Bayesian).
  • Explain sampling error, sampling distributions, standard error vs SD, the law of large numbers, and the central limit theorem.
  • Interpret confidence intervals and choose among z, t, χ², and F as the situation requires.
  • Compute and interpret normal-distribution probabilities in the statistical software.
  • Compute and interpret confidence intervals in the statistical software.

Week 13: The normal distribution and sampling

Work with the normal curve and sampling distributions that hypothesis testing relies on.

Week 14: Hypothesis testing

Hypothesis testing puts everything so far to use: state a claim, then test it against the data.

By the end of this week you can:

  • State null and alternative hypotheses and choose one- vs two-sided tests with appropriate critical regions.
  • Define Type I and Type II errors, alpha, and statistical power, and explain how changing alpha or n affects them.
  • Interpret p-values and “statistical significance” correctly, including what a p-value is not.
  • Interpret effect sizes (e.g., Cohen's d) and report a hypothesis test with the needed pieces.
  • Run and interpret a binomial test in the statistical software.
  • Run and interpret a one-sample t-test in the statistical software.
  • Read the software's hypothesis-test output and interpret the p-value in context.

Week 15: Apply hypothesis tests

Practice choosing the right test for a question and explaining the result.

Week 16: Comparing groups and relationships

The last module compares groups and relationships (t-tests, ANOVA, regression, chi-square), the tests you will meet in research.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Choose and interpret independent vs paired t-tests, state their assumptions, and use Levene's test when relevant.
  • Interpret Pearson r, r², and the sign of a correlation, and explain why correlation does not imply causation.
  • Match a research question to an appropriate test family (t-tests, chi-square, ANOVA, regression).
  • Interpret regression slopes, ANOVA, interactions, and the difference between statistical significance and effect size.
  • Run and interpret t-tests, chi-square, correlation, regression, or ANOVA in the statistical software.
