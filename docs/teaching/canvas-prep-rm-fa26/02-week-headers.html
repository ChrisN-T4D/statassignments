Week 1: Science of Psychology

This week you see why psychology counts as a science and how this course is organized, then read and acknowledge the syllabus.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Distinguish scientific, empirical, and falsifiable claims from common sense and pseudoscience.
  • Define theory and hypothesis and explain how theories are revised with evidence.
  • Identify confirmation bias and other practices that reduce biased reasoning.
  • Distinguish falsifiable scientific claims from unfalsifiable claims and avoid causal overreach from correlation alone.

Week 2: Scientific Method

You generate ideas and narrow a topic this week, which feeds directly into choosing your group and the topic workshop.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Outline the research process from topic to literature gap and rationale.
  • Use peer-reviewed literature (including Discussion sections and reference lists) to develop a project.
  • Align a proposal (question, design, measures, feasibility) and distinguish exploratory vs confirmatory work, including preregistration and secondary analysis.

Week 3: Research ethics

Read research ethics now so you can judge the ethics of the studies you review in your Article Review.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Apply Belmont principles (respect, beneficence, justice) and IRB review to human-subjects research.
  • Explain informed consent, deception, debriefing, and extra protections for vulnerable populations.
  • Describe confidentiality, ethical data handling, and how to reduce coercion in recruitment.

Week 4: Psychological measurement

This week builds the vocabulary for reading Method sections, which you use when you select a theory and review articles.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Define reliability and validity and explain how a measure can be reliable but not valid.
  • Classify scales of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) and how Likert-type items are often treated.
  • Identify forms of reliability and validity (test–retest, internal consistency, face, criterion, construct) and threats to construct validity.

Week 5: Experimental research

Learning to tell experimental from non-experimental papers prepares you to outline your literature review.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Identify IVs and DVs and distinguish random assignment from random sampling.
  • Define internal and external validity, confounds, and common threats to internal validity.
  • Compare between- vs within-subjects designs and controls (placebo, demand characteristics, carryover/counterbalancing).

Week 6: Non-experimental research

You learn to describe qualitative and observational studies, which you need to write the first draft of your literature review.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Interpret correlations without inferring causation (directionality and third variables).
  • Describe non-experimental methods (naturalistic and participant observation, qualitative, case study, archival, ethnography, content analysis).
  • Explain cross-sectional designs and when non-experimental work can still have strong external validity.

Week 7: Survey research

This week covers survey and sampling choices so you can describe the studies you cite as you revise your literature review.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Define sampling frame, probability sampling, coverage error, and nonresponse bias.
  • Write and evaluate survey items (double-barreled, open-ended, Likert, branching, order effects).
  • Identify response biases such as social desirability and practices that improve survey measurement quality.

Week 8: Quasi-experimental research

You learn to label quasi-experimental studies, which you need to finish your literature review synthesis.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Explain why quasi-experiments lack random assignment and compare one-group vs nonequivalent-control designs.
  • Describe interrupted time-series, regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences, and propensity-score logic at a conceptual level.
  • Identify quasi-experimental threats (including pretest sensitization) and use appropriately cautious causal language.

Week 9: Method choice

Comparing the four pathways this week prepares you to choose the method your project will use.

Week 10: Measures draft

You define your variables operationally and build your survey or protocol, which becomes your measures draft.

By the end of this week you can:

  • Define reliability and validity and explain how a measure can be reliable but not valid.
  • Classify scales of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) and how Likert-type items are often treated.
  • Identify forms of reliability and validity (test–retest, internal consistency, face, criterion, construct) and threats to construct validity.

Week 11: IRB first draft

This week you draft your IRB application, the document that must be approved before you can collect data.

Week 12: Methods section

Writing your Methods section now documents your design, participants, procedures, and measures for the project.

Week 13: IRB submission

You submit your IRB application this week; data collection cannot begin until it is approved.

Week 14: IRB processing

Use this week to track your IRB review status and plan data collection.

Week 15: Thanksgiving week

Week 16: Status update

Report your IRB status and your data collection plan, which closes out the fall capstone.
