The article review is not busywork. Each section trains a skill researchers use every week: finding literature, reading critically, and deciding whether your topic is too broad, too narrow, or ready for a problem statement.

1. Search terms & search log

Why it matters: Researchers rarely find six good articles on the first try. You build a search string, run it, learn from the hit count, and adjust.

When to narrow

Too many hits? Add a population, a specific construct, or a PsycINFO index term.

When to widen

Too few hits? Drop the strictest term, add synonyms (OR), or follow a citation from one good article.

2. Article cards

Why it matters: Each card is a reusable note. “What” and “why” train you to spot the authors’ question and gap; strengths and weaknesses teach critique, not summary.

When to narrow

Articles feel unrelated? Your topic may still be too broad — find a shared population or construct.

When to widen

Every article nearly identical? Try a neighboring construct or different population.

3. Source self-check

Why it matters: Not everything in a database is peer-reviewed original research. Look at the PDF before you invest time summarizing.

4. Problem statement

Why it matters: After several cards, patterns appear. “What we know / gap / what we want to know” forces synthesis — not copying one abstract.

When to narrow

Gap sounds like “everything about social media”? Name one IV, one DV, and one population you could study this semester.

When to widen

Cannot find a gap after six articles? Return to the search log — you may need more or different reading.

5. Early research question & hypothesis

Why it matters: Practice stating a testable direction now; you will revise after the full literature review. Design details belong in Phase 4.

When to narrow

RQ lists three constructs? Pick the relationship you care about most.

When to widen

RQ is vague? Add who and what context from your article cards.

What counts as peer-reviewed?

Use this while you evaluate each source. Methods Market does not decide for you — you look, then you check the boxes on each article card.

Usually peer-reviewed

  • Journal of ___ Psychology (or similar scholarly journal)
  • Volume + issue + page range or DOI
  • Method and Results sections with original data
  • Authors with university affiliations

Usually not acceptable for this assignment

  • News sites, blogs, Wikipedia, or general .com pages
  • Magazine or newspaper articles (Psychology Today, etc.)
  • Textbook chapters or encyclopedia entries
  • Opinion pieces with no Method section

Predatory journals exist. When in doubt, ask a librarian or your instructor before you spend time on the article.
