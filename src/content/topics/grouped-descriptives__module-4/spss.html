4.4 Descriptive statistics separately for each group

In many situations, you want to calculate descriptive statistics separately for different groups. For instance, you might want to know the mean salary for men and the mean salary for women, or the median age of people in different occupations. When you have a variable that splits the data into groups, you can use that variable to calculate statistics separately for each group. SPSS offers a few ways to do this, described below.

Terminology: Grouped Descriptive Statistics

Grouped (or stratified) descriptive statistics are calculated separately for different subgroups within your dataset.

  • Grouping variable – A categorical variable that defines subgroups (e.g., gender, treatment condition, occupation)
  • Factor / Split File – the SPSS settings that calculate statistics separately for each level of a grouping variable
  • Cross-tabulation (contingency table) – A table showing frequencies for combinations of two or more categorical variables

Key Concept: Comparing Groups

When you have a dataset with different subgroups, calculating statistics for the entire dataset might hide important differences between groups. By splitting your analysis by a grouping variable, you can:

  • Compare means, medians, or other statistics across groups
  • Identify patterns that only exist within certain subgroups
  • Check whether relationships differ by group membership

in SPSS, put your grouping variable in the Factor List of the Explore dialog, or use Data → Split File so every later analysis is run once per group.

4.4.1 Descriptives by group in SPSS

We will use the small sleep and stress file from Module 3 (five participants, Group coded 1 = Control and 2 = Treatment). There are two common routes.

Route 1: Explore. Choose Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Explore…. Move Stress into Dependent List and Group into Factor List. Under Display choose Statistics and click OK. SPSS prints one block of statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, IQR, skewness, kurtosis) for each group.

Route 2: Split File. Choose Data → Split File…, select Compare groups, move Group into Groups Based on, and click OK. Now run Descriptives or Frequencies as usual and SPSS produces the results once per group. Split File stays on until you turn it off. When you are done, return to Data → Split File… and choose Analyze all cases, do not create groups. A common mistake is forgetting this step and wondering why later results look odd.

The table below shows what the group statistics look like for the Stress scores (a very small, invented example):

Group N Mean Median Std. deviation
Control 3 10.33 11.00 2.08
Treatment 2 16.50 16.50 2.12

The Treatment group has the higher average stress (16.5 vs 10.3), and the spreads are similar (about 2.1 points). With only 2 and 3 cases per group we should not conclude anything from this; it is only to show how to read the table. A third option, Analyze → Compare Means → Means…, builds a compact table of chosen statistics by group (the Options button lets you add more).

4.4.2 Multiple grouping variables

You can also split by more than one variable. In Explore, put several variables in the Factor List (SPSS gives each its own set of results). In Split File, put two or more variables in Groups Based on and SPSS reports every combination, for example "Control, age under 21". The basic principle is simple: whenever you want statistics separately for different groups, name the grouping variable(s) in Explore or Split File.

4.4.3 Descriptives for categorical variables

When working with categorical (nominal or ordinal) variables, the statistics we've been discussing (mean, median, standard deviation) don't always make sense. For categorical data, frequency tables are often more useful. in SPSS, choose Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Frequencies…, move your variable into Variable(s), and click OK. This shows how many observations fall into each category, with percentages.

If you want counts for combinations of two categorical variables, choose Analyze → Descriptive Statistics → Crosstabs…, move one variable into Row(s) and the other into Column(s), and click OK. SPSS builds a cross-tabulation (also called a contingency table) showing the count for each combination. The Cells… button adds row or column percentages.
